Where modular fits in a data center project

Most people picture a modular data center as a box full of racks, and that's one use. In practice, prefabricated enclosures show up all over data center and power projects:

  • Edge and remote sites where a full building doesn't make sense, such as cell sites, campuses, factories and oil fields.
  • Electrical rooms and power skids housing switchgear, UPS systems and distribution, delivered as a finished room and set next to the main building.
  • Battery and UPS rooms, which often have their own fire and ventilation requirements and are easier to isolate in a separate structure.
  • Generator and fuel enclosures.
  • Network and telecom shelters.
  • Crypto mining and high-density compute, with either air-cooled units moving a lot of air or immersion tanks with dry coolers.
  • Capacity additions on existing campuses where adding a module beats expanding the building.

The common thread is equipment that needs a controlled room, delivered as a finished, inspected unit so site work is limited to foundations, utilities and connections.

Cooling decides almost everything

An IT or power enclosure is a heat problem first. Nearly every watt of power that goes into the equipment comes out as heat, and the room has to remove it.

ASHRAE's thermal guidelines for data processing environments are the usual reference. The recommended inlet air range for most IT equipment is about 18 to 27 °C (64 to 81 °F). Your design should hold that range on the hottest day at the site with one cooling unit out of service.

The common approaches, roughly in order of density:

  • Wall-mount air conditioners for low to moderate loads. They're simple, the equipment stays outside the room, and adding a redundant unit is straightforward.
  • Split or in-row DX cooling for higher densities, often with hot or cold aisle containment.
  • Chilled water where the site already has a plant.
  • Free air cooling for some mining and high-density compute setups, which needs serious filtration and a plan for humidity and dust.
  • Immersion, where the heat goes into fluid and out to dry coolers. The room itself carries a much smaller air load, but the floor carries a lot more weight.

Get the heat load in kilowatts, the target inlet temperature and the redundancy level settled first. The enclosure layout, the electrical design and the HVAC all follow from those three numbers.

Power and redundancy

Data center buyers usually describe redundancy in shorthand. N means just enough capacity, N+1 means one spare component, and 2N means two complete, independent systems. Each step up adds equipment, floor space and cost, so decide what the site actually needs. An edge site that can fail over to another location may be fine at N+1. A site with nowhere to fail over may need more.

Whatever the level, the enclosure has to make room for it: space for the UPS and batteries, a path for utility and generator feeds, an automatic transfer switch, and the NEC working clearances in front of every piece of gear. Bring the one-line diagram to the first conversation if you have it.

Batteries need their own planning

Battery rooms are where codes have tightened the most. Lithium-ion systems are typically evaluated under UL 9540 and the UL 9540A fire test method, and installed under NFPA 855, which covers spacing, capacity limits, ventilation, detection and suppression. Valve-regulated lead-acid batteries have their own ventilation requirements because they can release hydrogen.

That's one reason many owners put batteries in a separate enclosure from the rest of the gear. Isolation can simplify fire protection and protect the IT room if something goes wrong.

Fire protection and monitoring

NFPA 75 covers fire protection for IT equipment rooms. Many enclosures use a clean agent system designed under NFPA 2001, which puts out a fire without water damage, along with early warning smoke detection. The enclosure has to be tight enough to hold the agent concentration, so door seals and penetrations matter.

Remote sites also need eyes on them: temperature and humidity sensors, door contacts, leak detection, cameras and a connection to whatever building management or DCIM platform the owner runs. It's cheaper to plan the conduit and mounting points up front than to add them in the field.

Floors, structure and cable entries

IT and power equipment is heavy. A loaded rack can weigh well over 2,000 pounds, UPS and battery cabinets weigh more, and immersion tanks full of fluid are heavier still. Floor design should be based on the actual equipment, with point loads under cabinet feet and a plan for moving equipment in.

Decide early whether cables come in from above or below. Overhead tray keeps the floor clear and is common in enclosures. Bottom entry needs openings in the floor frame designed in from the start, not cut later.

Delivery, set and scaling

Data center enclosures follow the same transport limits as any modular unit. Loads wider than about 8.5 feet or taller than about 13.5 feet become oversize, so large rooms are often split into modules. Plan the crane, the lifting points and the foundation alongside the enclosure design.

One of the best reasons to go modular is that capacity can be added in steps. If you expect to grow, design the first module with the utility capacity, pad space and cable routing for the next one.

What to send for a quote

  • The equipment list with dimensions, weights and heat output.
  • The redundancy level for power and cooling.
  • The site location, so wind, snow and seismic loads and design temperatures can be set.
  • The one-line diagram, if you have one.
  • Fire protection and monitoring requirements.
  • Your target delivery date. Standard lead time for most projects is 8-12 weeks.

FAQ

Common questions

What is a modular data center enclosure?

It's a prefabricated room, usually steel-framed, that houses IT, power or cooling equipment and is delivered to the site finished and inspected. It can hold racks, switchgear, UPS systems, batteries or mining equipment.

How much cooling does a data center enclosure need?

Roughly as much as the power going into the equipment, since nearly all of it becomes heat. Add solar gain and lighting, then size for the hottest design day with at least one cooling unit out of service if uptime matters.

Should batteries go in a separate enclosure?

Often, yes. Lithium-ion battery systems fall under NFPA 855 and UL 9540A, with requirements for spacing, ventilation, detection and suppression. Isolating them can simplify fire protection and protect the rest of the equipment.

Can a modular enclosure be expanded later?

Yes, if you plan for it. Size the utility service, pads and cable routes for the future modules when you design the first one.
