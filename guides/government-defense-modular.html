Modular facilities for government and defense

What agencies and prime contractors buy, how the buying works, and the requirements that show up again and again in solicitations for modular buildings.

What government buyers use modular for

Federal, state and military buyers use modular facilities wherever they need secure, finished space without a long building program. Common uses include:

  • Secure storage and equipment enclosures.
  • Command posts, operations centers and offices.
  • Billeting and workforce housing.
  • Guard houses and entry control points.
  • Communications and IT shelters.
  • Training facilities, ranges and support buildings.
  • Restroom, shower and laundry facilities.

How the buying works

Federal opportunities are posted on SAM.gov, which is also where companies register to do business with the government. Registration gives a company a Unique Entity ID (UEI), and the Defense Logistics Agency assigns a CAGE code. Buyers search for suppliers by NAICS code; prefabricated metal buildings fall under NAICS 332311.

Many projects come through a prime contractor rather than directly from the agency. The prime holds the government contract and subcontracts parts of the work, which makes prime relationships just as important as direct bids for a supplier of modular buildings. Some contracts are set aside for small businesses or specific socioeconomic categories, and the solicitation says so up front.

Requirements that show up in solicitations

Every solicitation is different, but some requirements come up again and again:

  • Antiterrorism standards. For Department of Defense buildings, UFC 4-010-01, DoD Minimum Antiterrorism Standards for Buildings, sets standoff distances and construction requirements for inhabited buildings. It can affect where a building sits, its glazing and its structure.
  • Unified Facilities Criteria and Guide Specifications. Military projects often reference UFCs for design and UFGS sections for materials and workmanship.
  • Domestic content. The Buy American Act and, on some contracts, the Trade Agreements Act limit where materials and products can come from. Know which applies before you price.
  • Secure spaces. A Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility (SCIF) is governed by ICD 705 and its technical specifications, which cover construction, acoustics and access control. If a SCIF is in scope, involve the accrediting official early.
  • Codes and loads. Most projects reference the International Building Code and ASCE 7 for structural loads, often at a higher risk category for essential facilities.

Transportability

Some government units need to move by more than truck. Intermodal shipping by rail or sea uses ISO corner castings at standard dimensions, and containers in international transport need a CSC safety approval plate. Units intended for air transport have size and weight limits set by the aircraft. If a solicitation calls for any of these, they shape the entire design, so read those requirements first.

Submittals and documentation

Government projects run on paperwork. Expect a submittal register listing every document the contracting officer needs to approve: shop drawings, product data, structural calculations, test reports, a quality control plan, and warranty documents. Inspection records for each unit are often required at delivery. Suppliers who are used to commercial work sometimes underestimate this part. It should be priced and staffed from the start.

Working with primes

For a prime contractor, a modular subcontractor is only as good as their submittals, their schedule and their warranty. The things a prime looks for are clear: complete submittals delivered on time, a firm lead time in writing (ours is 8-12 weeks for most projects), and one accountable company standing behind the work after delivery.

FAQ

Common questions

What NAICS code covers modular buildings?

Prefabricated metal buildings and components fall under NAICS 332311. Some modular work is also classified under other construction and manufacturing codes, depending on the scope.

What is UFC 4-010-01?

It's the Department of Defense Minimum Antiterrorism Standards for Buildings. It sets standoff distances and construction requirements for inhabited DoD buildings, which can affect siting, glazing and structure.

Can a modular building be a SCIF?

Yes, with the right design. SCIFs are governed by ICD 705 and its technical specifications, and the accrediting official should be involved early in the design.

Do I need a prime contractor to buy modular facilities?

No. Agencies buy directly too. But many projects run through a prime contractor who subcontracts the modular scope, so suppliers work with both.
