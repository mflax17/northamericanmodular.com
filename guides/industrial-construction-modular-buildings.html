Modular buildings for industrial and construction sites

In-plant offices, site offices, guard houses, restrooms and equipment enclosures for plants and job sites, and the details that separate a building that works from one that gets in the way.

The usual list

Industrial plants and construction projects use modular buildings for the same reason: they need finished, usable space without tying up the site or the schedule. The common types are:

  • In-plant offices set inside a warehouse or production floor.
  • Construction site offices for the general contractor, owner's rep and subcontractors.
  • Guard houses and gate shacks.
  • Restroom and ablution blocks.
  • Control rooms overlooking a process or a yard.
  • Equipment and utility enclosures for compressors, pumps, electrical gear and controls.

In-plant offices

An office inside an existing building sounds simple, and most of the problems come from the building around it.

  • Fire sprinklers. A new roof inside a sprinklered warehouse can block the sprinklers above it. Under NFPA 13, the space under that roof may need its own coverage. Check with the building's fire protection engineer before you settle on a size and location.
  • Clearances. Measure the clear height to the lowest beam, sprinkler line or light, and leave room to set the unit with a forklift or crane.
  • Forklift traffic. Bollards or guardrails around the office keep a pallet from coming through the wall.
  • Noise. Production floors are loud. Insulated walls, solid doors and acoustic glazing make the office usable.
  • HVAC. The plant may be hot or unconditioned, so the office needs its own system and a place for the condenser.
  • Two-story units. A second level or roof deck for storage adds stairs, guardrails and real structural loads.

Construction site offices

A job trailer is used hard for a year or two, then moved. A few things make that easier:

  • A layout that fits the people who will actually use it: private offices for the superintendent and owner's rep, an open area, a plan table, and a conference room big enough for the weekly OAC meeting.
  • Durable floors and wall finishes, because people come in from the site in boots.
  • Tie-downs and anchorage for the site's wind speed.
  • Steps, landings and ramps that meet accessibility requirements.
  • Data and power planned for laptops, plotters, cameras and Wi-Fi.
  • Utilities that connect quickly: a single power feed, water and sewer stubs, or holding tanks where there's no service.

If you run a lot of projects, owning a small fleet of good units is often cheaper over time than renting tired ones project after project.

Guard houses and gate shacks

The guard has to see the gate, the approach and both lanes. That shapes everything: glazing on three or four sides, a counter at the right height, a transaction window or drawer, and a small HVAC unit that doesn't block the view.

If the site needs ballistic protection, bullet-resistant glazing and wall panels are rated under UL 752, which defines protection levels by the weapon and ammunition they stop. Decide the level with your security consultant; it changes the weight, the glazing and the cost.

Restroom and ablution blocks

Restroom buildings take more abuse than anything else on a site, so choose finishes that can be hosed down: FRP or aluminum wall panels, sealed floors with floor drains, and commercial fixtures. Plan for:

  • Fixture counts based on the number of people on site and local code.
  • Accessible stalls and clearances.
  • Exhaust ventilation and heat to keep the room dry.
  • Freeze protection on water lines in cold climates.
  • Water heaters sized for shift changes, if there are showers.

Equipment and utility enclosures

Compressors, pumps, chillers and electrical gear all do better in a controlled room. The enclosure specification starts with the equipment: dimensions, weight, heat output, noise, and the clearances needed to service it. Add ventilation or cooling for the heat, sound insulation if neighbors or workers are close, large doors or removable panels for equipment replacement, and a floor designed for the actual loads.

Planning the site

Most delays on modular projects come from the site, not the building. Before delivery, have the pad or foundation finished and level, utilities stubbed to the right locations, a clear path for the truck and crane, and someone on site who knows where the unit goes. Standard lead time for most of our projects is 8-12 weeks, which is usually enough time to get the site ready if you start at the same time.

FAQ

Common questions

Do in-plant offices need their own sprinklers?

Often, yes. A modular office's roof can obstruct the warehouse sprinklers above it, and NFPA 13 may require coverage under it. Confirm with the building's fire protection engineer before choosing a size and location.

Is it better to buy or lease a construction site office?

Leasing makes sense for a one-off project. Contractors who run several projects a year often save money owning a few good units, since they avoid ongoing rent and get a building set up the way they work.

What does UL 752 mean for a guard house?

UL 752 is the standard for bullet-resisting equipment. It rates glazing and panels by the level of protection they provide. Your security consultant should set the level, since it affects weight, cost and design.

What do I need on site before a modular building is delivered?

A finished, level pad or foundation, utilities stubbed to the right spots, clear access for the truck and crane, and a person on site to direct the set.
