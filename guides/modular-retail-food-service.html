Modular retail, food service and concession units

Why operators go modular

Retail and restaurant operators use modular units to open in places where a traditional building doesn't make sense: a parking lot, a stadium concourse, a new development that isn't finished yet, or a test market where they don't want to commit to a lease. A unit can be designed once and repeated, which helps brands that want every location to look and work the same.

The main types

  • Pop-up retail: storefront glazing, display space, a point-of-sale counter and storage.
  • Coffee and drive-thru kiosks: a compact service layout with one or two pickup windows, often with a walk-up window too.
  • Food-service shells: a finished, code-ready box with the rough-ins in place, so the operator's kitchen equipment can be installed.
  • Concession stands for stadiums, parks and event venues, built around roll-up service windows.
  • Cold storage: insulated, refrigerated units for food, floral or pharmaceutical storage.

What a food-service shell should include

The term shell means different things to different suppliers, so get it in writing. A useful food-service shell usually includes:

  • Washable interior surfaces, such as FRP or stainless, and sealed or epoxy floors with floor drains where the layout calls for them.
  • Plumbing rough-ins for sinks, a mop sink, a water heater and a connection to the grease interceptor.
  • Electrical service sized for the full equipment list, which is often larger than people expect once fryers, ovens, refrigeration and HVAC are added up.
  • A roof curb and structure ready for a kitchen hood and make-up air unit, if there will be cooking that produces grease.
  • HVAC sized for the kitchen heat, not just the floor area.
  • Service windows, doors and counters in the right places for the menu and the line.

Get the operator's kitchen equipment list before the design is final. Moving a sink or adding a circuit is easy on a drawing and expensive after fabrication.

Health department and local code requirements vary by city, so the operator's design team should confirm them before the drawings are finished.

Design and branding

A modular unit can look like anything. Exterior cladding, signage, awnings, lighting, canopies and color all help it read as a finished storefront rather than a box. For brands with multiple locations, a standard design saves money on every unit after the first and keeps the customer experience consistent.

Think about the customer side as carefully as the kitchen: where the line forms, where people wait for orders, shade and weather protection, and an accessible path from parking to the counter.

Cold storage

Cold storage units need more insulation than a standard building and a vapor barrier on the warm side to keep moisture out of the walls. Plan the target temperature first (cooler or freezer), then the insulation, refrigeration system, door seals and floor. Freezers need an insulated floor and a plan to keep the ground below from freezing and heaving. Temperature monitoring with alarms protects the inventory.

Placement and utilities

Most retail and food-service units sit on a simple foundation: concrete piers, a slab or a level gravel pad, depending on the unit and the site. Before delivery, plan the utility connections (power, water, sewer, grease interceptor and data), the drive-thru lane and stacking space if there is one, and the truck and crane access for the set. A unit designed to be moved later should keep its connections simple.

FAQ

Common questions

What is a food-service shell?

It's a modular unit finished to the point where the operator's kitchen equipment can be installed: washable surfaces, floor drains, plumbing and electrical rough-ins, and structure ready for a hood if needed. Get the exact scope in writing.

Can a modular unit be used as a drive-thru?

Yes. Coffee and quick-service brands use modular drive-thru units with one or two pickup windows. Plan the lane, the stacking space and the menu board along with the building.

Can modular retail units be moved?

Yes, if they're designed for it. Keep utility connections simple and use a frame that can be lifted and hauled without damage.

How do I make a modular unit match my brand?

Exterior cladding, signage, awnings, lighting and color can all be specified to match a brand standard. A repeatable design also lowers the cost of every unit after the first.
