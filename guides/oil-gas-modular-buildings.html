The buildings an energy site actually uses

A producing site, plant or pipeline station can need a surprising number of small buildings:

  • E-houses and MCC buildings holding switchgear, motor controls and drives.
  • Control buildings for operators, PLC and DCS equipment, and communications.
  • Analyzer shelters that keep instruments at a stable temperature.
  • Wellsite and field offices for company reps, engineers and supervisors.
  • Crew quarters, break rooms and restrooms.
  • Equipment and instrument enclosures.
  • Blast-resistant buildings for occupied spaces near process equipment.

Each of these has a different specification, but they share the same conditions: heat, cold, dust, corrosion, rough roads and, in many cases, the possibility of flammable gas nearby.

Start with hazardous area classification

Before anything else, find out whether the building sits in or near a classified area. In the U.S., petroleum facilities are classified under API RP 500 (the Class and Division system) or API RP 505 (the Zone system). The classification drawing tells you what kind of electrical equipment is allowed where.

There are three common ways to handle it:

  • Locate the building outside the classified area. This is the simplest and cheapest answer when the site layout allows it.
  • Use equipment rated for the area, which works for small instrument shelters but gets costly for a full building.
  • Purge and pressurize the building under NFPA 496, so clean air from a safe source keeps it at positive pressure, with gas detection and shutdowns if pressure is lost.

The area classification belongs to the operator and their engineer. The building supplier needs it at the start, because it changes the HVAC, the electrical design and sometimes the location of the building on the pad.

Blast resistance and facility siting

If people will occupy a building near process equipment, the operator's facility siting study decides whether that building needs to resist blast, fire or toxic release. API RP 752 covers permanent buildings and API RP 753 covers portable buildings, which includes most modular and skid-mounted units.

Blast-resistant modules are specified by the overpressure and duration they need to withstand and the level of damage that's acceptable. They use heavier steel walls, blast-rated doors and windows (or none), and anchorage designed for the load. They're a specialized product. If your siting study calls for one, share the study's blast loads with the supplier at the first conversation.

Designing for the climate

Energy sites run from West Texas summers to North Dakota winters, and the building has to work in both.

  • Heat and sun: size the HVAC for the design high at the site, use reflective roofing and good insulation, and plan for redundancy where equipment is critical.
  • Cold: heat strips or unit heaters, freeze protection on any plumbing, and insulation that keeps electronics above their minimum ratings.
  • Dust and sand: filtered air, positive pressure and good door seals keep it out of the electronics.
  • Corrosion: coastal and sour gas sites need coated steel, corrosion-resistant envelopes and hardware rated for salt spray.
  • Wind: structure and anchorage designed for the site's wind speed under ASCE 7.

Skids, moves and relocation

Many energy buildings move during their life, from one pad to the next as drilling moves or a field develops. If that's likely, design for it:

  • A skid base with lifting points and, if needed, tow points.
  • A structure stiff enough to be picked and hauled more than once without cracking finishes.
  • Quick-connect utility points and tie-downs that can be reused.
  • Dimensions that stay within normal trailer limits (about 8.5 feet wide and 13.5 feet tall loaded) so moves don't require oversize routing.

Plan for relocation from the start. A building that wasn't designed to move often gets damaged the first time it does.

E-houses in more detail

An E-house is a prefabricated electrical room, delivered with the gear either installed or ready to receive it. The specification follows the same rules as any electrical room: NEC working clearances in front of the equipment, doors at each end for large gear, outswing doors with panic hardware near equipment rated 800 amps or more, floors designed for the equipment weight, and cooling sized for the heat the drives and transformers give off.

Coordinate early on cable entry (top or bottom), the location of utility and generator connections, and whether the gear will be set before or after the building is delivered.

What to have ready for a quote

  • What the building will be used for, and how many people will occupy it.
  • The site's hazardous area classification drawing, if the building is near process equipment.
  • Facility siting study results, if blast resistance is a question.
  • The site location and climate extremes.
  • An equipment list for electrical and control buildings.
  • Whether the building will be relocated, and how often.

FAQ

Common questions

What is an E-house?

An E-house is a prefabricated electrical room that houses switchgear, motor control centers, drives and controls. It's delivered as a finished unit and set on a foundation or skid at the site.

What standard covers blast-resistant modular buildings?

API RP 753 covers the siting of portable buildings, which includes most modular units, at process plants. API RP 752 covers permanent buildings. The operator's facility siting study sets the blast loads the building needs to resist.

Can a modular building go in a hazardous classified area?

Sometimes. The options are to rate all electrical equipment for the area, or to purge and pressurize the building under NFPA 496. Locating the building outside the classified area is usually simpler and cheaper.

Can wellsite buildings be moved between pads?

Yes, when they're designed for it, with a skid base, lifting points, a stiff frame and dimensions that stay within normal trailer limits.
