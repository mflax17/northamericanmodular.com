Electrical enclosures for pump stations

What goes into an enclosure that keeps a pump station's drives, controls and switchgear running, and the decisions you want settled before anything goes to fabrication.

What the enclosure is really protecting

At most pump stations the pumps get the attention, but the electrical enclosure is what keeps them running. It usually houses the motor control center, variable frequency drives, the main breaker or switchgear, the PLC and SCADA panels, and often the automatic transfer switch for the standby generator. If water, heat or corrosion takes out that room, the pumps stop, usually during the storm the station was designed for.

That is why a pump station enclosure should be treated as a piece of critical infrastructure and specified like one. A shipping container with a door cut in it will hold equipment. It won't necessarily keep that equipment cool, dry and serviceable for 30 years next to a wet well.

Start with the equipment list and the heat load

Everything else follows from what's going inside. Before layout or HVAC can be designed, the engineer needs the equipment list with dimensions, weights and heat output.

Drives are usually the biggest heat source. A drive running at around 97 percent efficiency gives off roughly 3 percent of its power as heat. So 150 kW of drives puts about 4.5 kW of heat into the room, which works out to around 15,000 BTU per hour, or about 1.3 tons of cooling, before you count the sun on the roof, lighting, transformers and control panels.

A few things we see get missed:

  • Redundancy. If losing one air conditioner means the drives trip on high temperature, plan for two units sized so either one can carry the load (N+1), with a lead/lag controller that alternates them.
  • Design temperature. Size cooling for the hottest days at your site, not an average day. Drive manufacturers publish a maximum ambient rating, and that number is the ceiling.
  • Heat in winter. Cold-climate stations need heat to keep electronics above their minimum rating and to control condensation. Wall-mount units with electric heat strips handle both.
  • Filtration. Dust and corrosive air go wherever the air goes. Filtered, slightly positive-pressure air keeps more of it out.

Wall-mount units like the Bard units on our sample specification are a common choice because they keep the cooling equipment outside the room and are easy to service.

Get the floor above the flood

Stormwater and flood-control stations are, by definition, in places that flood. The enclosure floor should sit above the design flood elevation for the site, and many owners add freeboard on top of that. ASCE 24, Flood Resistant Design and Construction, is the standard most engineers work from, along with local floodplain rules.

In practice that usually means setting the enclosure on concrete piers or a steel platform, with stairs and landings to the doors. It also means thinking about every opening below that line: conduit entries should come through sealed penetrations, and anything that can't be raised, like an underground duct bank, needs a plan for keeping water from following the conduit into the gear.

Plan for corrosion from day one

Wastewater stations produce hydrogen sulfide, and even clean-water and stormwater sites are wet most of the time. Bare carbon steel doesn't last in that environment.

Some of the choices that matter:

  • An aluminum exterior envelope over a coated steel frame. Aluminum won't rust, and 3003-H14 sheet holds up well in wet, mildly corrosive air.
  • Coated steel for anything structural, with attention to welds and cut edges, which is where coatings fail first.
  • Stainless or coated fasteners rated for salt spray, not plain zinc.
  • Siting the enclosure upwind of, and away from, the wet well vent.
  • Filtered intake air, so the HVAC doesn't pull corrosive gas across the electronics.

Know where the classified areas are

NFPA 820, the Standard for Fire Protection in Wastewater Treatment and Collection Facilities, classifies areas around wet wells and other spaces where flammable gas can collect. Electrical equipment inside a classified area has to be rated for it, which gets expensive quickly.

The usual approach is to keep the electrical enclosure outside any classified area with physical separation. When that's not possible, a purged and pressurized enclosure, designed under NFPA 496, is one option. Either way, the area classification is the engineer of record's call, and it should be settled before the site plan is final because it decides where the enclosure can go.

Leave room to work on the gear

The National Electrical Code sets working space in front of electrical equipment, and an enclosure has to be laid out around it. Under NEC 110.26, the depth of working space for equipment between 151 and 600 volts to ground runs from 3 feet to 4 feet depending on what's on the opposite wall, with at least 30 inches of width and 6.5 feet of headroom.

Two other rules shape the box itself:

  • Larger equipment, rated 1,200 amps or more and over 6 feet wide, needs an entrance to the working space at each end.
  • For equipment rated 800 amps or more, personnel doors within 25 feet of the working space have to open outward and carry listed panic hardware.

Those rules are why many pump station enclosures end up with doors at both ends, swinging out. It's worth sketching the clearances on the floor plan early. Adding 2 feet of length on paper costs very little. Finding out at inspection that a door is in the wrong place costs a lot.

Structure: wind, snow and heavy floors

Pump stations are public infrastructure, and ASCE 7 treats them that way. Water and wastewater facilities commonly fall under Risk Category III, and pump structures needed to maintain water pressure for fire suppression can fall under Risk Category IV. The higher the risk category, the higher the design loads.

The floor is where enclosures most often get under-designed. MCCs and switchgear are heavy and concentrated. The stormwater project on our specifications page was designed for a 250 psf floor live load plus equipment loads, with 124 mph wind at Exposure C and a 30 psf roof snow load. Your numbers will be different, but they should come from your site, and they should be on stamped calculations from the engineer of record.

Anchorage matters just as much as the frame. The connection between the enclosure and the foundation is what resists uplift and sliding in a storm, so ask to see how it's detailed.

Getting it to the site

A finished enclosure has to travel. Units wider than about 8.5 feet or taller than about 13.5 feet on the trailer become oversize loads, which brings routing restrictions and escorts. That doesn't rule out larger enclosures, but it should be part of the design conversation. Long rooms are sometimes split into two modules joined on site.

Plan the set as well: crane size and reach, lifting points designed into the frame, a clear path for the truck, and a foundation that's finished and checked before delivery day. Our standard lead time for most projects is 8-12 weeks, and the foundation work can usually happen in the same window.

What to ask any supplier for

  • Confirmation that the unit follows the project's stamped structural plans, including the frame, envelope, connections and anchorage.
  • Shop drawings showing the equipment layout and NEC working clearances.
  • The HVAC sizing basis: heat load, design temperatures and redundancy.
  • Materials and finishes, especially for anything exposed to corrosive air.
  • An inspection record for the finished unit.
  • The warranty, in writing, and who stands behind it.

FAQ

Common questions

Can a shipping container be used as a pump station electrical room?

It can, but it usually takes so much modification (insulation, new openings, reinforcement for floor loads, corrosion protection) that a purpose-designed enclosure is the better value. Container steel is also thin and rusts quickly in wet, corrosive air.

How big should a pump station electrical enclosure be?

It depends on the equipment list and the NEC working clearances in front of it. Lay out the gear first, add the required working space, then size the enclosure around that, leaving room for future equipment if you expect the station to grow.

What risk category applies to a pump station enclosure?

Under ASCE 7, water and wastewater facilities commonly fall under Risk Category III, and pump structures that maintain water pressure for fire suppression can fall under Risk Category IV. The engineer of record makes the final call for each project.

Do pump station enclosures need air conditioning?

Almost always, if they house drives. Drives give off a meaningful share of their power as heat, and most are rated for a maximum ambient temperature. Redundant wall-mount units with electric heat are a common setup.
